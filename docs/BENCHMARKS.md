# Benchmarks

All numbers below were **measured**, not estimated, by `backend/scripts/benchmark.py` on 2026-11-20.

## Method

- **Input:**
  - Nine synthetic 30-second stereo files with known damage, generated by
    `python scripts/benchmark.py --synthetic`. No copyrighted audio. The base signal is stereo
    noise with a pink (-3 dB/octave) tilt and drum-like transients. It is a stand-in for music, not
    music, and is about 13 dB peak-to-loudness, like a typical mix.
  - For the de-clipping accuracy test, also a **30-second excerpt of a real song** from the
    project owner's local test set. It is not included in the repository.
- **Pipeline:** `enhance()` exactly as the worker runs it, with the Spotify profile
  (-14 LUFS, -1 dBTP), at 48 kHz and 24-bit.
- **"Before"** is the gatekeeper on the input. **"After"** is the gatekeeper run again on the
  delivered 24-bit WAV, and the output true peak is measured with 16x oversampling.
- **"Changed beyond volume"** is the output minus the best pure-volume copy of the input, in dB
  relative to the output. It shows how much the processing really altered the audio, ignoring the
  loudness change. As a rough guide, about -20 dB is an obvious change, about -40 dB is subtle,
  and below about -60 dB is practically inaudible; it depends on the music.
- **De-clipping accuracy** compares the repaired audio with the **known original** before clipping,
  on the samples clipping destroyed. "-6 dB" means the error is halved; positive means worse than
  leaving the clipping alone.
- **Machine:** Apple M4 (10 cores), macOS, Python 3.11.15. The default device was `mps`.
  **AudioSR was not available.**
- **Reproduce:**
  `cd backend && python scripts/benchmark.py --synthetic --no-env`, plus
  `--truth-pair CLEAN.wav CLIPPED.wav --boost-db N` for your own clean/clipped pair.

## Results: de-clipping accuracy against the known original

The de-clipper was replaced on 2026-11-20. The earlier cubic-spline repair was measured on the
same material and **made light clipping on real music worse**, so it was swapped for
**A-SPADE**, a sparse de-clipper from the research literature (Kitić et al. 2015; Záviška et al.
2019). A-SPADE rebuilds each flattened peak as the sparsest spectrum that keeps every unclipped
sample unchanged and stays at least as tall as the flat top.

| Material | Samples destroyed | Cubic spline (old) | **A-SPADE (current)** | A-SPADE speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Real song, boosted +3 dB and clipped | 3.2% | **+2.1 dB (worse)** | **-3.0 dB** | 6.8x real time |
| Real song, boosted +6 dB and clipped | 10.4% | -2.1 dB | **-6.1 dB** | 4.9x real time |
| Real song, boosted +9 dB and clipped (`test_clipped.wav`) | 21.6% | -3.8 dB | **-7.2 dB** | 4.3x real time |
| Synthetic, +6 dB (benchmark files 02 and 03) | 0.3% | +0.1 dB | **-1.4 dB** | 24x real time |
| Synthetic, clipped and cut at 11 kHz (file 06) | 0.3% | n/a | **-3.3 dB** | 24x real time |
| Synthetic, +9 dB | 3.7% | -0.2 dB | **-2.0 dB** | 5.3x real time |

**How to read this:**
- **Better in every case.** A-SPADE moves the destroyed samples closer to the original than the
  spline did in every case measured, and never further away. Samples that weren't clipped are
  returned bit-for-bit unchanged; a unit test checks this.
- **Heavy clipping is only partly repaired.** On the heavily clipped song (21.6% of samples
  destroyed), the error drops by 7.2 dB, so less than half remains. That's an audible improvement,
  but not a return to the clean original.
- **Light clipping changes little.** The repaired peaks are a small fraction of the track, so the
  whole-track change is about -40 dB. That's subtle, and expected.

## What changes, and what doesn't

Measured with the same "changed beyond volume" method on files from the local test set:

| File | Change beyond volume | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A clean master (`01_clean_master`) | -43 dB | Repair is skipped. Only the limiter touches a few peaks |
| Lightly clipped (`02_hard_clipped_+6dB`) | -40 dB | Only the rebuilt peaks change, about 5% of the time |
| Heavily clipped real song (`test_clipped.wav`) | about -11 dB | Large, audible change; 98% of it on the clipped peaks |
| Two band-limited MP3 files (`test_lowbitrate.mp3`, `bad.mp3`) | **about -150 dB (no change)** | **Only the volume changes.** Their only damage is missing highs, and restoring those needs AudioSR, which isn't installed |

So on this setup:
- **Healthy and MP3-damaged tracks** come out sounding essentially like the input at a new loudness.
- **Clipped tracks** come out with partly rebuilt peaks.

That's the honest scope: the dashboard reports "Highs not restored" and the job outcome is
`partial`.

\* **Case 06 needs care.** Its after-analysis reports a 21.0 kHz cutoff and a 100 A score.
Those highs were **not** restored: de-clipping adds broadband energy above the original 11 kHz
cliff, so the cliff detector no longer finds a 20 dB drop. That energy is a by-product, not
music. The dashboard's "after" value for high frequencies says **"Not restored"** instead of the
measured number, and the job outcome is `partial`.

## Not measured (yet)

- **AudioSR quality and speed.** It needs a CUDA host. Our adapter feeds it 5.12 s chunks
  (AudioSR's recommended segment length) with 50 diffusion steps, and its real-time factor on a
  given GPU must be measured before any cost or latency claim.
- **Listening tests.** The accuracy figures are waveform error against a known original. Blind
  A/B listening on real material is still needed before claiming a *perceptual* improvement.
- **Real-world clipping.** Clipping that was later smeared by a lossy codec (an MP3 of a clipped
  master) has no flat tops, so it isn't detected and isn't repaired.
- **Throughput under concurrency.** These are single-process, one-file-at-a-time timings.
